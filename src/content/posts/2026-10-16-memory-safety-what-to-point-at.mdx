The memory-safety question has changed shape. It used to arrive in conference talks and blog arguments, where the answer could be a position. Now it arrives in vendor security questionnaires and supplier assessments, where the answer has to be a list of things you actually do.

C++26 did not settle it. The profiles work is still moving, [contracts are being argued about](/posts/contracts-dispute/) rather than deployed, and neither of them is in a compiler you can ship against this quarter. So the honest answer to "what is your memory-safety story" is not a standard feature. It is a set of things that already work.

## What you can point at today

**A hardened standard library, switched on in production.** libc++ and libstdc++ both ship modes that turn out-of-range container access and similar precondition violations into a trap rather than undefined behaviour. The cost is the part people assume is prohibitive and is not: [measured across Google's fleet at roughly 0.3%](/posts/hardened-stdlib-turn-it-on/). That is a number you can put in a questionnaire, and it covers a large share of the bug class being asked about.

**Sanitizers in CI, not just on a developer's laptop.** AddressSanitizer, UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer and ThreadSanitizer find the bugs the type system does not. They are too slow for most production builds and entirely affordable on a test suite. ["We run ASan and UBSan on every pull request" is a concrete control](/posts/sanitizers-on-ce/); "we are careful" is not.

**Warnings promoted to errors, including the ones about buffers.** Clang's `-Wunsafe-buffer-usage` is the mechanism behind Chrome's spanification work, which reached [97% compliance while fixing over a thousand security bugs](/posts/chrome-spanification/). Whatever you think of the migration cost, it is a measurable programme with a number attached.

**Types that make the bug unrepresentable.** `std::span` instead of pointer and length. `std::string_view` where lifetimes allow. C++26's `std::indirect` for the [PImpl case](/posts/std-indirect-pimpl/). None of this is new, and all of it removes a category rather than checking for it.

## What not to claim

Do not claim profiles. They are not in a shipping compiler, the framework paper is still being revised, and asserting otherwise in a security document is the kind of thing that surfaces badly later.

Do not claim contracts as a safety feature. They are in the C++26 draft, GCC implements them, and [a group including Stroustrup is arguing they should be removed before the final ballot](/posts/contracts-dispute/) partly on the grounds that they do not deliver safety guarantees. Whatever the outcome, "we use C++26 contracts" is not an answer to a memory-safety question today.

Do not claim the compiler catches it. The [float-to-int conversion case](/posts/float-to-int-ub/) is a good reminder: undefined behaviour that no warning fires on, in code that looks obviously fine, caught only by a sanitizer flag that GCC does not include in `-fsanitize=undefined`.

## The shape of an honest answer

An inventory of where memory-unsafe patterns live, a hardened build for the parts that ship, sanitizers gating the test suite, warnings-as-errors on new code, and a migration plan for the highest-risk components. Everything in that list exists now and none of it depends on the standard settling its argument.

The reason to write it down is not compliance theatre. It is that the alternative answer, that C++ is memory-unsafe and always will be, is the one being written down for you.
